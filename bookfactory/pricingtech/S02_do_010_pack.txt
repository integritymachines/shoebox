# BOOK — Pricing Tech

blurb:
  Pricing Tech follows a technology firm's pricing across three stages: founder judgment under thin data (Strategy), written rules a human still owns (Policy), and an auditable engine that maps the environment to a real-time price vector once item-by-item control fails (Engine). Freemium, early-bird windows and list tiers are read as choices under scarcity and interaction rather than as folklore. [Intro/Journey L465–467]
  The book is written for technical founders. It sits above MBA and Silicon Valley treatments, introduces formal methods only where a stage earns them, and points outward instead of deriving them in full. It also tracks how digital, information and attention goods change which constraints bind without abolishing economics. [Intro/Mindset Gap L473; Intro/Information Goods L477–479]

abstract:
  The introduction sets out a growth path in which prices move from hand-set judgment to debatable rules to a supervised, auditable engine, and states the end-state as a specification of what that engine must do and what Strategy and Policy must feed it [Journey L465–467]. It names a mindset gap: academic models assume clean demand, measured costs and identified rival reactions that early firms lack, while founder heuristics are dismissed as under-theorized [Mindset Gap L471–473]. A second line of argument contrasts the production economy with digital, information and attention goods, where marginal cost is often a boundary case and capacity appears as compute, compliance or support [Information Goods L477–479]. Method: light notation, integrity framed as efficiency and accountability, Carnegie bounds on rationality, serialized self-contained sections, no footnotes [Method L483–485].

# PART I — Strategy
abstract:
  Part I reconstructs the economic reasoning beneath founder-set prices under thin data and a boundedly rational founder [Strategy Stage L492–496]. It treats labels such as freemium, early bird, tiers, meters and seat licenses as bundles of decisions about who pays, when price rises and what is rationed [Hidden Choices L500–504]. Six chapters build this out: Foundations, Principles, Value, Interaction, Uncertainty, Behavior [Chapter Map L508–514]. Part I stops where choices must become explicit, revisable rules, which is Policy [Strategy Scope L518–520].

## Ch1 — Foundations (intro only; sections held with A)
abstract:
  Foundations fixes the primitives later arguments depend on: the priced object, the cost concept that binds when copies are cheap, how digital and attention goods alter the production account, and who pays whom on a platform [Primitives L527–531]. It separates "copies are cheap" from "serving a customer is free," since cost-to-serve often binds after reproduction cost stops [Object, Cost L535–537]. On platforms, the price on one side changes the value of participation on the other [Goods, Platforms L541–543]. It does not derive willingness to pay, rivalry or policy [Prior Ground L549].

## Ch2 — Principles
abstract:
  Principles offers rules of thumb that survive thin data: opportunity cost, locating the binding margin, commitment and credibility along price paths, and separating heuristics with economic content from imitation [Thin-Data Rules L568–572]. It treats early-bird and freemium as opportunity-cost problems first [Opportunity, Margins L576–578], and price paths as time-consistency problems [Commitment, Heuristics L582–586]. It stops before Value, Interaction and Policy [Rule Limits L590].

  - Every price is chosen against a named alternative: another segment, waiting, or declining the sale. [Opportunity, Margins L576]
  - An early-bird offer that trains buyers to wait for the next one has bought the wrong option. [Commitment, Heuristics L582]

### 2.1 Opportunity
  The relevant comparison for a deal is the next-best deal, not doing nothing; the scarce resource is often sales time or support slots rather than the product [Alternatives L596–600]. The linear-programming shadow price gives the idea a precise form, zero when a constraint is slack [Shadow Prices L604–608]. Under thin data it is bounded with crude proxies, a satisficing search in Simon's sense, and forgone options should be written down because ledgers omit them [Proxies L612–614].

### 2.2 Margins
  Near-zero marginal cost holds for reproduction and often fails for service; the answer depends on the unit sold [Binding Cost L620–622]. Capacity turns marginal cost into a step function, and human and compliance capacity usually bind before servers, illustrated by a support engineer handling about two hundred accounts [Capacity L626–632]. Where marginal cost really is near zero, it sets only a floor, and price follows value to the buyer, the argument of Information Rules [Boundary Cases L636–638].

### 2.3 Commitment
  A price is a point on a path buyers and rivals project forward; Kydland and Prescott's time inconsistency and Coase's 1972 durable-goods conjecture frame the problem [Price Paths L644–646]. Credibility comes from costs of deviating, and buyers' beliefs set the realized path [Credibility L650–654]. Early-bird offers work when the window is finite and credible; lock-in gains are partly prepaid at entry; sunk promises buy credibility at the cost of flexibility [Early Commitments L658–662].

### 2.4 Heuristics
  A portable rule names its alternative, the cost it protects and a consistent price path; Cyert and March explain why weak rules persist [Portable Rules L668–672]. Freemium is economic only under margin and value conditions; tiers should follow the binding margin; annual-prepay discounts are bounded by cost of capital and churn [Freemium, Tiers L676–680]. Folklore such as "three tiers always" spreads by imitation; keep heuristics whose rationale can be written and drop the rest [Folklore L684–686].

## Ch3 — Value
abstract:
  Value covers willingness to pay without a demand curve, creating versus capturing value, integrity along multi-step chains, and the division of surplus under thin data [Exchange L693–695]. Willingness is a disciplined conjecture formed about a boundedly rational buyer; capture is limited by complements, spillovers and bargaining position [Willingness, Capture L699–701]. Integrity means efficiency and traceable contribution, and surplus is tracked as an economic quantity [Integrity, Surplus L705–711]. Rivals and platform games are left to Interaction [Value Edges L715].

  - A product can create large value and capture little; added value against substitutes, under the conditions Brandenburger and Stuart state, bounds what it can capture. [S3.2 L749–751]
  - A link that over-extracts in a multi-step chain can shrink the chain that pays it. [S3.3 L791–793]

### 3.1 Willingness
  Young firms lack the data to identify a demand curve, but every price embeds a willingness hypothesis that should be written down with the buyer's alternative [Without Curves L721–725]. Stated preference (Van Westendorp, conjoint) informs rankings more than levels; revealed preference, a term from Samuelson, is costly and confounded [Stated, Revealed L729–733]. Segments defined by alternative, Simon's satisficing buyer and an invented 60-trial example show how sparse evidence bounds the hypothesis, recorded as it is revised [Sparse Evidence L737–743].

### 3.2 Capture
  Creating and capturing value are separate; Brandenburger and Stuart's added value bounds what a firm can capture, under conditions they state [Create, Capture L749–753]. Teece's complementary assets send value to owners of the scarce complement, spillovers should not be counted as chargeable, and Arrow's information paradox makes trials a disclosure cost [Complements L757–761]. Bargaining room lies between willingness and cost; the Nash solution is a reference point, and an invented 120,000 contract example shows which alternative sets the ceiling [Bargaining Room L765–769].

### 3.3 Integrity
  Integrity is an economic property of the chain: price structures such as per-call meters and seat prices induce behavior, and effort spent contesting division is deadweight [Efficiency L775–779]. Following Alchian and Demsetz, untraceable contributions cannot be rewarded, so traceability precedes sustainable capture, and broken firm commitments draw down willingness [Traceability L783–787]. An invented chain example shows extraction at one link shrinking surplus downstream; the frame does not forbid high prices backed by added value [Chains L791–795].

### 3.4 Surplus
  User surplus is willingness minus price and firm surplus is price minus opportunity cost; their sum is the value created. Marshall named consumer surplus, a concept Dupuit had developed earlier, and it remains the most direct statement of what a buyer keeps [Two Surpluses L801]. User surplus drives renewal and referral, and a discount is productive only when the transferred surplus buys something the firm values [Two Surpluses L803–805]. Thin data make the firm's own share easier to see than the buyer's, so decisions drift toward extraction; tracking proxies and recording the assumed split limit the drift [Split Bounds L809–813]. A division is sustainable when each side keeps enough surplus to prefer continuing, which reduces the fairness frame to the integrity conditions [Exchange Terms L817–821].

## Ch4 — Interaction
abstract:
  Interaction drops the passive-demand reading: buyers anticipate and withhold, rivals respond on observable instruments, and platform terms set who pays whom [Other Agents L828–830]. A price justified without a strategic buyer or a rival has not entered the chapter's subject [Other Agents L830]. It names buyer and rival behavior without claiming an identified model [Buyers, Rivals L834–836], prices platform sides as an instrument for participation, and names only the strategic forms the firm can populate [Sides, Games L840–844]. Surplus accounting, Part II playbooks and Engine simulation stay out [Interaction Scope L848].

### 4.1 Buyers
  Buyers read a posted price as one observation of a policy; early-bird windows pull purchases forward and quarter-end discounts push them back [Anticipation L854–858]. Menus are search problems, and published lists, free tiers and past contracts become reference prices, shown in an invented 50-list, 35-close example [Search, Reference L862–866]. Strategic silence biases interviews and trials; posted prices with self-selection and usage components move the information problem rather than remove it [Strategic Silence L870–874].

### 4.2 Rivals
  Cournot, Bertrand and Kreps–Scheinkman differ in what each firm holds fixed, which matters more than the number of rivals; founders can still write down a reaction map [Reaction L880–884]. Matching and undercutting are ambiguous signals; undercutting pays only when the rival cannot follow, as an invented 30-to-20 cut shows, and price-matching promises can soften competition [Undercutting L888–894]. Hotelling-style differentiation is the escape from price fights, chosen because a rival exists [Escape L898–902].

### 4.3 Sides
  Rochet and Tirole define two-sided markets by price structure, not only level, so one side's price cannot be judged against that side's cost alone; Foundations named platforms as the case where who pays whom becomes a design variable [Price Structure L908–910]. The side that pays is usually less price-sensitive; per Armstrong, single-homing sides are competed for and multi-homing sides can be charged more [Price Structure L912]. Cross-subsidy is an investment in participation justified only by revenue on the paying side [Cross-Subsidy L916]. Neither side wants an empty platform; Caillaud and Jullien treat this as a coordination problem solved by subsidizing one side enough that it joins whatever it expects the other to do and recovering the subsidy on the other side, which they call divide and conquer [Cross-Subsidy L918]. An invented scheduling-platform example has free listing earning 80,000 against 60,000 [Cross-Subsidy L920]. Terms such as exclusivity, anti-steering and data access allocate surplus and draw less scrutiny than fees [Platform Terms L924–930].

### 4.4 Games
  Schelling's commitment, Dixit's capacity-backed deterrence and Milgrom–Roberts limit pricing are the useful names at founder scale; an incumbent with fixed renewals may be unable to match a newcomer [Founder Scale L936–940]. Repeated interaction, via folk theorems, makes restraint and reputation valuable, and one-shot reasoning misleads when agents return [Repeated Play L944–948]. Name a form when players, instruments and the sign of response are known; record the rest as deferred [Deferred Forms L952–958].

## Ch5 — Uncertainty
abstract:
  Uncertainty treats beliefs before identification, sparse signals, irreversible moves as options, and decisions with an empty spreadsheet [Beliefs First L965–967]. A prior is an explicit stance on what is unknown; signals need separation from noise when samples are small [Beliefs, Signals L971–973]. List cuts and discount windows consume flexibility, and transfer by analogy must not be presented as identified econometrics [Costly Reversal L977–981]. Policy experiments and Engine scenario libraries stay out [Information Scope L985].

### 5.1 Beliefs
  A price set before demand is measured still rests on a belief; following Bayes and Savage, a prior is a written, coherent statement that later evidence can move, while Knight's distinction marks that early pricing sits closer to uncertainty than to risk, so a prior commits the firm to consistency, not accuracy [Explicit Priors L991–993]. A usable prior has a central belief, a spread and an update list [Explicit Priors L995]. Perceived quality and demand at scale are separate unknowns, and early adopters are a selected group whose willingness should not be extrapolated [Unknown Quality L999–1003]. Bayes's rule exposes over- and under-reaction; an invented example moves a weight of one half to about two thirds after three of ten trials convert [Revision Rule L1007–1009]. Sales-call statements and promotion-era demand warrant low weight, set in advance [Revision Rule L1011].

### 5.2 Signals
  Early evidence arrives as fragments with several explanations, and buyers may speak strategically to shape their quote [Noisy Feedback L1017–1019]. With twenty trials, three versus six conversions is within chance range for many plausible rates [Noisy Feedback L1021]. Cohorts separate price from value problems better than conversion counts but are confounded by channel, version and season, as an invented March/May example shows [Cohort Scraps L1025–1029]. Selection limits what conversion measures; Spence's signaling explains hesitancy to price low, with limits since price is cheap to change [Selection L1033–1035]. Evidence is ranked in advance: unprompted buyer behavior highest, confounded cohorts middle, negotiation statements lowest [Selection L1037].

### 5.3 Options
  Dixit and Pindyck show that irreversible, delayable investment carries an option value of waiting, but in early pricing the firm often learns only by selling, so delay helps only when uncertainty resolves independently [Waiting L1043–1047]. Moves range from private quotes to grandfathered contracts in reversibility; flexibility is worth more when beliefs are wide, illustrated by starting at 60 with private discounts versus publishing 30 [Irreversibility L1051–1055]. Discount windows combine irreversibility with teaching, as in the Coase problem; each should state what it is expected to teach and what result ends it [Discount Windows L1059–1063].

### 5.4 Thin Data
  Analogy to neighboring prices, costs and buyer budgets is legitimate when assumptions are explicit; several anchors, with their disagreements recorded, beat copying one rival [Analogy L1069–1073]. Beliefs are stated as ranges; an invented plan of 80 x 5% x 2,000 = 8,000 spans roughly 1,000 to 64,000 when each input is uncertain by a factor of two, yet a position must still be chosen and held [Ranges L1077–1081]. Three decision rules: weigh asymmetric error costs, prefer informative instruments, set a review date and update list; none guarantees the right price under Knightian uncertainty [Decision Rules L1085–1087]. The chapter's minimum standard: stated prior, evidence ranking, account of flexibility spent, and a chosen position within a range [Decision Rules L1089].

abstract:

Attention binds before capital, so menu design and process design are joint problems [Bounds, Attention L1102–1104]. Early teams price by exception with rationale held in chat and memory; Discipline hands Policy heuristics with stated rationale and revision thresholds [Organizations, Discipline L1108–1112]. Governance matrices and automation stay out [Carnegie Scope L1116].

### 6.1 Bounds
  Simon's procedural rationality, judging the reasonableness of the process given available information, is the standard Behavior applies to founder pricing, and the earlier Part I chapters already meet it [Procedural Reason L1122–1124]. The Carnegie tradition concerns limits on search and routines, not a catalogue of anomalies [Procedural Reason L1126]. Aspiration levels rise after success and fall after failure; Cyert and March's problemistic search explains menus built from local fixes, and explicit triggers move the argument before a shortfall [Aspiration Levels L1130–1134]. Prospect theory and the 1986 Kahneman–Knetsch–Thaler survey give a direction of effect, not a design rule, so reference dependence is a constraint on paths [Reference Points L1138–1142].

### 6.2 Attention
  Following Simon's 1971 remark that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention", founder attention binds first, so the right procedure is one the team will actually execute, and a coarse rule followed consistently yields better evidence [Founder Load L1148–1150]. Revision is rare because its cost is immediate and its benefit diffuse [Founder Load L1152]. Menus carry cognitive costs on both sides; separation buyers cannot perceive does not separate, and an invented five-plan menu consumes thirty founder-hours per fifty deals [Menu Costs L1156–1160]. Buyer search cost shapes demand and is set by free tiers, trials and gated quotes; signals gathered under different search costs are not evidence about the same willingness [Buyer Search L1164–1168].

### 6.3 Organizations
  Early firms price by exception; in March and Simon's terms they lack programs for a recurring decision, and exceptions accumulate experience without a record [Exception Culture L1174–1178]. Authority diffuses unassigned; uncertainty absorption passes inferences in place of evidence, and Cyert and March's coalition view explains menus reflecting which group decided; assigning authority is left to Policy [Who Decides L1182–1186]. Williamson's incomplete contracts make renegotiation a source of exceptions, and delegation to resellers moves pricing to people with different information and goals [Firm Boundaries L1190–1194].

### 6.4 Discipline
  A stated rationale names the instrument and rule, the economic reason, the evidence and its strength, and the boundary, and exposes rules resting only on imitation or analogy [Stated Rationale L1200–1204]. Revision triggers are set at adoption, on the upside as well as the downside; an invented startup half-off rule carries two triggers that evidence can contradict [Revision Triggers L1208–1212]. Part I hands Policy a set of rules with reasons, evidence, boundaries and triggers, beginning with Optimization's grammar [Policy Handoff L1216–1220].

# PART II — Policy
abstract:
  Part II makes pricing decisions explicit while a human still owns them, because written rules economize on scarce attention across a growing organization [Explicit Rules L1227–1231]. Policy states objectives, constraints and instruments, then writes segmentation, differentiation, competitive stance and learning as rules [Policy Content L1235–1241]. The six chapters run Optimization, Governance, Segmentation, Differentiation, Competition, Learning in order of dependence [Part Sequence L1245–1249]. Autonomy is excluded by design [Policy Limits L1253].

## Ch7 — Optimization
abstract:
  Optimization is the grammar of Policy: Objectives, Constraints, Instruments, Control [Grammar L1260–1262]. Objectives are arguable aims with named multi-goal tensions; constraints such as margin floors and capacity belong in the model [Stated Aims L1266–1268]. Instruments are mapped to those aims, and Control keeps override rights and the duty to record reasons with people [Levers L1272–1276]. Authority and automation come later [Grammar Limits L1280].

### 7.1 Objectives
  Defensible rules can still conflict, so Policy starts by writing an objective that names a quantity and a direction [Success Measures L1286]. Revenue, contribution and account counts each imply a different best price; taking from Principles that a pricing decision is judged at the margin against the next-best alternative and the binding cost, the firm states which version governs [Success Measures L1288]. A useful objective is measurable, attributable and contestable; "capture fair value" fails, while contribution from new annual contracts subject to floors passes [Success Measures L1290]. Every objective has a horizon, usually an unstated current quarter; naming it turns later effects into terms, and omitting a lifetime term sets the future's value to zero [Time Horizons L1294–1298]. Multiple aims are normal: Pareto-efficient policies form a frontier that analysis can locate but not choose on, and weights or floors make the choice explicit, as in an invented 400-per-account discount ceiling [Pareto Tension L1302–1306].

### 7.2 Constraints
  A constraint is honored or broken while an objective is traded at the margin, so the choice of form is a policy decision [Margin Floors L1312]. A defensible margin floor states which cost it protects, on which unit, and why [Margin Floors L1314]. Floors need a scope: a contract-level floor permits a low-priced component that carries another, a structure Interaction showed can be efficient when one component or side draws participation for another [Margin Floors L1316]. Promise integrity covers grandfathered rates, most-favored-customer clauses, published lists and revenue shares; regulatory envelopes and capacity limits complete the list [Promise Integrity L1320–1322]. Lagrange multipliers, extended to inequality constraints in conditions now credited to Karush (1939) and Kuhn and Tucker (1951), are the shadow prices of 2.1 and show what each constraint costs; an invented capacity limit of 20 accounts implies about 15,000 per slot and a 75,000 hiring threshold [Lagrange Multipliers L1326–1330].

### 7.3 Instruments
  Four levers, list, discounts, meters and bundles, carry the objective; freemium, early bird and annual prepay are configurations of them [List Anchors L1336]. List is the anchor, moved least often because it touches promise constraints, and it signals position to rivals [List Anchors L1338–1340]. Discounts are legitimate when a rule names the objective term and constraint they serve and carries a revision trigger; authority is left to Governance [Discount Rules L1344–1346]. The meter defines the unit of payment and is often a larger decision than the price per unit; Principles argued that a good meter tracks either the buyer's benefit or the firm's binding cost, and the stated objective decides which governs when the two diverge; two-part structures, a fixed fee plus a usage charge, and Oi's 1971 "Disneyland dilemma" analysis show how an entry fee and a per-use price capture surplus a single price leaves behind [Meters, Bundles L1350]. Bundles, per Adams and Yellen (1976), raise profit by reducing dispersion in willingness to pay but can breach line-item floors [Meters, Bundles L1352]. The levers interact, as an invented meter rise combined with a wider volume discount shows [Meters, Bundles L1354].

### 7.4 Control
  The model ranks and proposes; a named person decides and the record shows whether the proposal was followed [Model Aid L1360]. Poor identification and bounded attention make the model an aid, and Simon's procedural standard makes the process inspectable [Model Aid L1362–1364]. Overrides are recorded as data in the grammar's terms; an invented 40 percent override rate citing implementation cost reveals a missing cost term [Override Rights L1368–1372]. A model that cannot be explained cannot be overridden on reasoned grounds; openness means plain objectives, listed active constraints, named instruments and ranked evidence, and Governance supplies the owners next [Open Models L1376–1380].

## Ch8 — Governance
abstract:
  Governance assigns owners to the grammar across four sections, 8.1 Authority, 8.2 Audit, 8.3 Exceptions and 8.4, the last treating integrity as an operating constraint [Owners L1387–1389]. Different instruments need different owners, and audit means a price's rationale can be reconstructed [Authority, Audit L1393–1395]. Exceptions carry expiry and pattern detection; integrity, the frame Value defined and Optimization applied, now binds rights and escapes as governance [Exceptions, Integrity L1399–1403]. Segments and stance are not designed here [Owner Scope L1407].

### 8.1 Authority
  A decision right is a named role's standing permission to change a specific price field within stated limits, with a duty to record it [Decision Rights L1413]. Jensen and Meckling's agency costs explain why rights are divided, and Fama and Jensen's split of decision management from control shows why one seat should not propose, approve and review [Decision Rights L1415–1417]. Instruments need different owners; an invented deal desk reviews 10 of 200 deals, 5 percent, under 10/20/30 percent bands [Deal Desk L1421–1425]. Aghion and Tirole's formal versus real authority shows approval drifting to the informed agent; clear rights protect speed [Escalation Paths L1429–1433].

### 8.2 Audit
  Audit records, for each change, the field, values, role and right, accounts, objective term or constraint served, and ranked evidence, plus any model proposal [Change Records L1439–1443]. One record must serve both the sales view by account and the finance view by instrument; Kerr (1975), in "On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B," describes reward systems that pay for one behavior while the organization hopes for another, and the record exposes the pattern [Two Views L1447–1451]. Decisions are judged against what was known, policy disputes go to the policy owner, and audit shows agreement between rationale and move rather than causation, as in an invented 24 of 30 untested competitive claims [Disputes L1455–1459].

### 8.3 Exceptions
  Exceptions are needed because policy is written in advance, but unlimited ones dissolve policy; a well-formed exception has separate requester and approver, a grammar-stated reason, and an expiry [Escape Valves L1465–1469]. Expiry reverses the default, should match the reason, and bounds the cost of error, which makes speed and expiry complements [Expiry Dates L1473–1477]. Patterns separate revision from drift, as an invented seat-minimum waiver held by 25 of 120 accounts shows; review needs a threshold and an owner [Drift Signals L1481–1487].

### 8.4
  Section 8.4 treats institutional integrity as agreement between stated policy and practice; Brunsson (1989) describes stable separation of talk and action, and inconsistency gives the model a false feasible set [S8.4 L1493–1497]. Change, exception and override records allocate accountability internally, the governance counterpart of Alchian and Demsetz's metering, while fields no review uses should be removed [S8.4 L1501–1505]. The constraint binds on authority, audit and exceptions and is checked by comparing stated and realized terms, as in an invented 33 of 150 contracts (22 percent) discounted without policy [S8.4 L1509–1513].

### 9.1 Observables
  A segment is usable only when membership can be decided from a trait recorded at quote time; observables are the signals stable enough to define a group in advance [Visible Marks L1546–1548]. The inventory sorts traits into recorded, recorded but manipulable, and informal; only the first supports a segment without a fence or verification [Visible Marks L1550]. Observables are proxies with error in both directions; an invented 1,000-employee threshold is right for 300 of 400 accounts (75 percent) yet finds only 300 of 550 high-budget accounts (about 55 percent) [Proxy Error L1554–1556], and each proxy carries a review date [Proxy Error L1558]. The permitted list is itself a policy under a named owner; traits buyers see as irrelevant damage reference points, and each hypothesis names observable, direction, size and evidence [Privacy Bounds L1562–1566].

### 9.2 Discrimination
  Pigou's The Economics of Welfare (1920) classified price discrimination into three degrees, which modern usage has extended: second degree, in the modern sense, is a common schedule that buyers sort into, whereas Pigou treated it as a stepwise approximation of the first; the degrees map onto segment lists, quantity and term schedules, and individual negotiation [Three Degrees L1572]. In economics, discrimination means price differences not explained by cost differences, and most schedules mix cost and willingness [Three Degrees L1574]. Recorded, hard-to-fake traits support direct group pricing; otherwise a schedule of quantity breaks and term discounts lets choices reveal type, with version menus left to Differentiation and individual negotiation at the costly end [Three Degrees L1576]. Arbitrage takes the forms of misreporting, resale or sharing, and changing the trait; fences limit it at their own cost [Arbitrage L1580–1582]. An invented leakage case, 20 of 200 accounts at a gap of 40, costs 800 against 12,000, about 6.7 percent, so Governance can set a tolerance [Arbitrage L1584]. Segment pricing is accepted when its basis is visible and tied to the transaction [Fair Treatment L1588]; the book treats fairness through integrity rather than ethics, with efficiency and accountability for contribution as Value defined it and policy-practice agreement as Governance added, so a segment policy needs a written, consistently applied basis that survives audit, and a relationship discount denied to an identical buyer fails that standard [Fair Treatment L1590]. Unexplained differences become negotiating material [Fair Treatment L1592].

### 9.3 Cost-to-Serve
  Contribution is price minus serving cost, which varies across segments as much as willingness does [Serving Costs L1598]. Activity-based costing (Cooper and Kaplan) and its time-driven variant (Kaplan and Anderson) are used only to test whether a segment's cost differs enough to change its floor or eligibility [Serving Costs L1600]. Margins, in Principles, observed that marginal cost depends on the unit sold; segments consume different infrastructure units, and where costs scale with usage a flat price leaves margin exposed to heavy users, remedied by a usage component, cap or eligibility rule [Serving Costs L1602]. Freemium is a segment priced at zero; an invented free tier of 50,000 users costing 240,000 a year against 300,000 of conversion contribution clears by 60,000 and breaks even at 0.8 percent conversion [Free Volume L1606–1608]. Unbilled overage and out-of-plan support are cost lines in the segment record [Free Volume L1610]. Ranking segments by contribution, not list price, corrects effort; a 30 percent premium tracking a 30 percent cost gap rests on a different argument than one with equal cost [Joint Margins L1614–1616], and cost estimates carry stated error [Joint Margins L1618].

### 9.4 Codification
  A written segment states name, observable, verification, instrument, fence, cost assumptions, owner and review date, joining Optimization's grammar with Governance's rights [Written Segments L1624–1626]. New segments must show a difference large enough to justify overhead, and boundary rules settle disputes once [Written Segments L1628–1630]. Deal hygiene codes each quote with its segment and any exception, and states reclassification rules in advance; in an invented quarter, 60 of 240 deals are uncoded, so the 15 percent exception rate covers only three quarters of the book [Deal Hygiene L1634–1638]. Folklore is restated as hypotheses and kept or dropped; exception clusters and review dates drive revision on a stated cadence [Rule Revision L1642–1646].

### 10.1 Lines
  A line lets buyers sort themselves within a list; vertical lines rank by agreed quality and horizontal lines offer variety buyers disagree on [Quality Ladders L1679–1681]. Mussa and Rosen (1978) show a monopolist lowering the quality of lower steps to reduce high-valuation buyers' surplus, possibly leaving the lowest types unserved [Quality Ladders L1683]. In an invented two-type ladder, redesigning Basic from 100 to 70 for high types lets Pro rise from 140 to 170 [Step Design L1687]. A step is a fence only if it changes something the high type values more and Audit can see; ladders of three or four steps are common, and unused steps are removed [Step Design L1689–1691]. Variants carry their own cost-to-serve, variety for one deal is an exception in product form, and line changes sit with Governance's decision rights [Line Ownership L1695–1699].

### 10.2 Versioning
  Shapiro and Varian set out versioning as a menu built from one information good for self-selection [Feature Gates L1705]. Gates run on features, volume, delay, fidelity and support; they work where they track the buyer's stakes, follow recorded observables, and are enforced in the product [Feature Gates L1707–1709]. Deneckere and McAfee (1996) documented damaged goods that cost as much or more to make, and showed such damage can be a Pareto improvement over a single high price when it brings in low-valuation buyers [Damaged Goods L1713]. Mussa and Rosen explain why damage pays, and Value's integrity requires the record to name the fence any costly damage protects [Damaged Goods L1715–1717]. Versions fail by pooling or by heavy users choosing the low version; volume gates belong in the definition, and each version states its intended buyer, gate and review trigger [Pooling Risk L1721–1725].

### 10.3 Bundles
  Taking the Adams and Yellen result from Optimization as given, the question is which form to offer [Mixed Bundling L1731]. Adams and Yellen showed that mixed bundling, suitably priced, does at least as well as pure bundling and, since separate sales are also a special case of it, as well as selling components alone; an invented three-buyer case lifts revenue from 200 to 290 by adding X alone at 90, assuming indifferent buyers buy [Mixed Bundling L1733]. The component price is itself a fence [Mixed Bundling L1735]. Add-ons cover combinations with few priced objects and need contract-level floor scope; metered add-ons apply Oi's two-part logic when use drives cost and varies widely, and add-ons should be few [Add-Ons L1739–1743]. Bakos and Brynjolfsson (1999) show large bundles of near-zero-cost goods make valuations predictable, a case that weakens with real component cost; suites need an internal allocation, and a one-deal bundle is an exception [Large Bundles L1747–1751].

### 10.4 Imitation
  Menus are visible and their shape is cheap to copy, but not the economics behind it; fences held by shape alone erode first [Copied Menus L1757–1761]. Installed base and switching costs, which Principles treated under lock-in, make a step valuable to existing buyers in ways a new rival cannot replicate, and use data, certifications and lower cost-to-serve also travel slowly [Durable Assets L1765]. Important steps belong on asset-based attributes; an invented 80,000 feature earning a 30,000 quarterly premium pays only with a lead of three quarters or retention, so the assumed lead is written down [Durable Assets L1767–1769]. A review cadence, such as quarterly evidence and annual structure with a Governance exception path, checks separation, rival gates and cost, and its record keeps lead-time estimates honest [Refresh Cadence L1773–1777].

### 11.1 Rivalry
  The stance starts from a rival list naming the few sellers that appear in the firm's evaluations and lost-deal records [Rival Map L1812]. For each it records the instrument moved, the response lag, slow-variable position and apparent objective as dated hypotheses, and marks which evidence is public and which anecdotal [Rival Map L1814–1816]. A response rule fixes trigger, instrument, band, duration and owner, as in an illustrative 90-day mid-market match within band [Response Rules L1820]; consistent responses teach rivals what a cut costs, and no rule should commit to matching a lower-cost rival [Response Rules L1822–1824]. Non-response rules name moves the firm will not answer, with evidence and review date, and the stance is judged by whether each quarter's rules can be explained, not by an estimated equilibrium [Holding Still L1828–1832].

### 11.2 Entry
  Baumol, Panzar and Willig's contestable markets (1982) are a reference point: the nearer a segment is to free entry, the less a price above cost can persist [Contestable Margins L1838]. An inventory of exposed margin separates cheap-to-reproduce work from asset-protected margins [Contestable Margins L1840]. Bain (1956) classified entry conditions as blockaded, effectively impeded or ineffectively impeded, which later work reads as blocking, deterring and accommodating entry, and Sylos-Labini set out the limit-price assumption [Contestable Margins L1842]. Klemperer (1987) shows sellers competing for new buyers and pricing above cost to locked-in ones [Switching Costs L1846]. Switching costs protect the installed base but cap renewal extraction; in an invented case a 50,000 account facing a 38,000 entrant and 10,000 a year of switching cost leaves the buyer 2,000 a year better off after switching, so the account is at risk because the entrant's annual saving exceeds the buyer's annualized cost of switching by 2,000, and a 5,000 renewal increase widens that to 7,000 [Switching Costs L1848]. Fudenberg and Tirole (1984) name incumbent postures [Accommodation L1852]; accommodation, stated in writing, is often the integrity-consistent choice when capacity binds, and limit pricing is an insurance premium to be stated and reviewed [Accommodation L1854–1856].

### 11.3 Softening
  Softening lowers each seller's gain from a pure price fight, using Differentiation's structures and the firm's pricing rules [Matching Clauses L1862]. Salop (1986) explains how meet-competition and most-favored-customer clauses reduce the gain from undercutting; their legal side is left to Enterprise, but each such term is recorded and scrutinized [Matching Clauses L1864–1866]. Doom loops arise with slack capacity, near-zero incremental cost and bookings-based pay; in an invented case three matched 10 percent cuts take contribution from 32 to 21.16, while a one-round band caps the loss at 4 per seat [Doom Loops L1870–1872], and repeated temporary matches are drift [Doom Loops L1874]. Identity-driven matching is checked by named owners, stated reasons and comparison of matched with unmatched deals [Ego Checks L1878–1882].

### 11.4 Contests
  Contests are chosen, bounded price fights; each has a written objective, target segment, instrument, budget in forgone contribution, dates and owner, with the end date communicated so it does not become the reference price [Promotion Design L1888–1890]. A rival's response is evidence for the rival map [Promotion Design L1892]. Share is worth what later contribution it brings [Share, Margin L1896]; in an invented win-back at 25 percent off a 20,000 contract, an account yields 17,400 over two years against 22,400 at full price, so the offer pays only on accounts that would not otherwise have come, measured against a holdout [Share, Margin L1898–1900]. Success and failure exit criteria are written at the start, and the contest record feeds Learning; the war-chest narrative does not survive such a record [Exit Criteria L1904–1908].

### 12.1 Experiments
  An experiment is the one source where the firm controls who sees which price [Affordable Tests L1943]. Tests carry revenue, operating and attention costs, so the question is which uncertainty would change a large decision [Affordable Tests L1945]; in an invented case 600 trials a month at 5 percent need about 13 months to reach 200 conversions per arm, leaving three honest choices [Affordable Tests L1947]. Metric, population, stop rule and decisions are written before launch [Test Hygiene L1951]. Armitage, McPherson and Rowe (1969) show that stopping at the first favorable look inflates false positives; Thompson's 1933 proposal began bandit allocation, which cuts test losses but weakens the losing-arm estimate [Test Hygiene L1953]; eligibility must be fixed in advance [Test Hygiene L1955]. Irreversible moves, published promises and small samples limit randomized tests [Untestable Moves L1959–1963].

### 12.2 Updating
  Updating treats each result as an addition to a written belief [Light Posteriors L1969]; in an invented case a prior worth 400 trials at 5 percent and a week of 6 in 60 pool to about 5.7 percent, and stating the prior's weight exposes it to criticism in both directions [Light Posteriors L1971–1973]. Overfitting is limited by pre-named comparisons, pooling small cells and checking results across metrics, and successes are reviewed alongside failures [Overfitting L1977–1981]. A change note records evidence, prior, revision size, residual uncertainty, reversal condition, owner and review date, and a sequence of notes shows convergence or oscillation [Change Notes L1985–1989].

### 12.3 Memory
  Following Levitt and March (1988), learning is encoded into routines, so memory keeps the rationale as well as the rule, including null results and decisions not to act [Retained Rationale L1995–1999], selected by what a successor would need [Retained Rationale L2001]. Turnover converts unwritten exceptions into loss and deepens Levitt and March's competency trap; records belong to roles, with handover of open reviews [Turnover L2005–2009]. Retrieval links each rule to its history in a shared structure that a person can query and, in Part III, a system can read [Retrieval L2013–2017].

### 12.4 Loops
  A loop is a schedule and trigger set that brings each rule back for review [Revision Cadence L2023]. Cadence differs by rule, from annual review of objectives to monthly review of discount bands with event triggers, and every review ends confirmed, revised or retired on the record [Revision Cadence L2025–2029]. Argyris and Schön (1978) distinguish single- from double-loop learning, and the annual review asks whether the objectives themselves still hold [Double Loops L2033–2035]; March (1991) on exploration and exploitation motivates a budgeted share of exploratory tests [Double Loops L2037]. Engine inherits learning rules as inputs without authority to rewrite them, and double-loop decisions stay with people [Engine Handoff L2041–2045].

### 14.1 Movement
  Inputs drift between proposals, and transient and persistent shifts carry different error costs [Drifting Inputs L2248–2250]. Staleness cost accumulates silently; in an invented cost rise from 60 to 66 on 1,000 units a month, monthly review saves 12,000 against 8,000 of review cost, while a rise of 2 would favor quarterly review, since staleness scales with drift and lag but review cost does not [Staleness Cost L2256–2260]. ### 14.2 Path Dependence
  The effect of a move depends on earlier moves, some costly to undo [Lock-In L2274]. David (1985) and Arthur (1989) gave the term its form for technologies and markets; the book applies it to a firm's dependence on its own installed commitments [Lock-In L2276], with the installed base as the main carrier of history [Lock-In L2278]. Price cuts and learned waiting are ratchets; in an invented case a temporary cut of 10 on 100 loses 5,000 on its own terms and a further 5,000 each later quarter if it teaches 500 buyers to wait [Ratchets L2282–2286]. Legacy terms such as grandfathered tiers and price locks are carried as owned constraints; under integrity as Value and Governance established it, removing them is a breach, and changes route through Authority and Audit [Legacy Terms L2290–2294].

### 14.3 Stability
  Ezekiel's 1938 cobweb theorem describes lagged responses that circle an equilibrium; the book applies the lag structure to one engine's price responses [Cobweb Cycles L2300]. A rule that raises or cuts price on lagged conversion gaps can oscillate, and oscillation teaches waiting, invites matching and defeats tracing [Cobweb Cycles L2302–2304]. Damping shrinks responses; in the invented linear case a response multiple of 0.5 converges, 1.5 converges with sign flips, and 2.5 diverges, so strength starts low with the lag in view and alternating deviations are reported [Damping L2308–2310]. Move bounds and dead bands add quiet, a frequently binding bound signals its shadow price, and the stability-responsiveness tradeoff is stated per cadence class and recorded [Move Bounds L2314–2318].

### 14.4 Feedback
  Feedback concerns which signals the loop listens to, how fast and with what weight [Signal Choice L2324]. Each signal is named with its relation to the objective, since conversion alone misleads, and mechanically recorded signals are preferred to ones passed through interested parties, as with Kerr's folly [Signal Choice L2326–2328]. Signals arrive with different lags, biasing a naive engine toward moves whose costs arrive late; in an invented smoothing case a weight of 0.2 moves an estimate 6 on a 30-point jump instead of 30 [Lags L2332–2334]. Kalman's 1960 principle, weight rising with estimate uncertainty and falling with observation noise, is borrowed, with weights stated and recorded [Lags L2336]. Following Forrester and Sterman, rules are reviewed with their loops; feedback changes prices within bounds but never bounds or objectives, which change only through Learning and Governance [Policy Gates L2340–2344].

### 15.1 Conjectures
  A conjecture is a written claim about how buyers or rivals respond to an observable move, and every engine carries one stated or not [Stated Responses L2379]. Each conjecture carries a falsifier, as in an invented rival that matches in only one of four cuts and is retired; parameter updates are single-loop, retirement goes through Governance, and a short owned register beats a long unchecked one [Falsifiers L2393–2397].

### 15.2 Simulation
  Simulation scores alternatives against the conjecture register before commitment, and is worth most where reversal is costly and response uncertain [Rehearsal L2403–2405]. In the invented running case, a cut of 5 on a 100 list gains 2,000 if rival B holds and loses 3,250 if B matches, so it pays only if B holds with probability above about 0.62, turning the decision into a question about one conjecture [Rehearsal L2407]. Counterfactuals compare alternatives and isolate price effects, start from the firm's existing commitments, and test the engine's damping and the risk of learned coordination before live use [Counterfactual Runs L2411–2415]. The compute and attention budget is set by approval class, results are reported as ranges naming the driving conjectures, and regime breaks pass to scenarios [Compute Budget L2419–2423].

### 15.3 Scenarios
  Scenarios are coherent alternative market states, run against the policy with stated input values [Stress Cases L2429]. Following Wack (1985) and Schwartz (1991), the method is narrowed to pricing policy over quarters [Stress Cases L2431]. In the running case a 30 percent demand collapse leaves a 2,000 shortfall against 30,000 of fixed cost, and a 10 percent cut would need full volume recovery, so holding price while cutting serving cost is stronger [Stress Cases L2433]. Regime scenarios replace sets of conjectures that fail together, flag rules that depend on one regime, and pre-state what the engine does when a regime signal fires [Regime Shifts L2437–2439]. Scenarios attach to decision gates, fit on one page, and stay few [Decision Use L2443–2447].

### 15.4 Auditability
  Every proposed price must be explainable from the record: inputs, rule, conjectures, binding constraints with shadow prices, approval class and approver [Explained Prices L2453–2455], so account managers can defend it [Explained Prices L2457]. Inputs are versioned, conjectures and scenarios enter the trail so retired assumptions can be traced to affected prices, and responses to rival prices are logged [Input Trails L2461–2465]. Rudin (2019) argues for interpretable over post hoc explained models in high-stakes use; the book adopts interpretable rules where they perform within tolerance and confines opaque components to inputs [Black Boxes L2469–2471]. Intelligence remains an aid under Control [Black Boxes L2473].

### 16.1 Multi-SKU
  Catalog items share buyers, references and resources, so the unit of analysis is the portfolio and the output a price vector; per-item pricing errs predictably by counting diverted buyers as gains [Catalog Unit L2506–2508]. Baumol, Panzar and Willig's economies of scope make per-item margins depend on an allocation rule [Catalog Unit L2510]. Demand links run through buyers and constraint links through the firm, and they need different evidence [Coupling Links L2514–2518]. An invented 300-item catalog has 44,850 possible pairs, so the engine encodes a few strong clusters, records omitted links, and adds clusters only when the gain covers estimation and review [Complexity Tax L2522–2526].

### 16.2 Cannibalization
  Cannibalization is a cross-price effect among the firm's own substitutes; the multiproduct pricing condition raises prices among substitutes and lowers them among complements, needing only direction and rough size of strong links [Cross-Effects L2532–2534]. Paths follow Differentiation's lines: trade-down, trade-up, free-tier diversion and cross-region leakage [Trade Paths L2538]. Moorthy (1984) explains trade-down under self-selection; the book widens the term [Trade Paths L2540]. In an invented case a Basic cut gains 2,500 alone but loses 8,000 on Pro, a net 5,500 loss [Trade Paths L2542]. The diversion ratio, here 0.4, is the most usable proxy and gives a screen whose break-even is 0.125; scoring items alone assumes every ratio is zero [Diversion Proxies L2546–2550].

### 16.3
  Section 16.3 treats shared limits such as blended margin commitments, shared support budgets and tier-ordering promises, which make feasibility a property of the whole vector [S16.3 L2556–2558]. Joint costs mean a per-item floor encodes an allocation rule, while the actual commitment is usually to a total [S16.3 L2560]. A separate invented illustration, not the 16.2 case (Basic 40 at variable cost 22, Pro 100 at variable cost 40), meets a 50 percent blended floor at about 51.4 percent although a per-item floor would reject Basic [S16.3 L2564]; a trade-down of 100 accounts with no price changed drops it to about 49.7 percent, so the constraint layer needs Cannibalization's cross-effects [S16.3 L2566]. Ramsey (1927) and Boiteux (1956) give the rule for independent demands that markups, as a share of price, vary inversely with elasticity, applied here to a private contribution floor [S16.3 L2568]. ### 16.4 Price Vector

### 17.1 Limits
  People can make only so many price decisions well in a period; following Simon, the ceiling is set by attention, and it binds through exception arrivals, catalog size and environmental speed, which compound [Human Ceiling L2637–2639]. Little's law (1961) turns queue complaints into an identity: in an invented case 40 exceptions a day waiting three days means 120 open, and utilization rising from 0.8 to 0.96 lengthens waits far more than arrivals rise [Exception Load L2643]. Capping the queue only moves waiting into unrecorded concessions; arrivals fall through codification and waits through approval classes [Exception Load L2645–2647]. In an invented catalog, 2,400 cells at 5 percent weekly drift need 120 reviews, rising to 720 with faster drift and twice the regions; cadence classes become a capacity plan, and automation widens where waiting costs more than expected automation error [Catalog Speed L2651–2655].

### 17.2 Throughput
  Throughput is the rate at which justified price changes reach the market, limited by serial steps computation cannot shorten [Serial Steps L2661]. Borrowing Amdahl (1967), an invented cycle with 20 percent serial approval speeds up at most fivefold however fast the engine runs [Serial Steps L2663]. Approval classes become routine, review and escalation lanes, each with an owner, entry rule, capacity and a recorded default on silence [Approval Lanes L2667–2669]; Little's law applies within each lane [Approval Lanes L2671]. An internal service level for prices caps staleness and challenge response, and a missed level is information for Learning's revision rules [Price Service L2675–2679].

### 17.3 Markets
  Rollout is sequenced so evidence can be attributed and failures contained, each market starting in review with its own kill switch, beginning with markets that resemble home and allow error [Rollout Order L2685–2689]. Constraints localize, so shared inputs are owned centrally and local floors, envelopes, stances and promises are owned per market [Local Bounds L2693–2695]. Integrity applies in each market on the same terms, and a firm that states one policy globally and practices another locally fails the third condition [Local Bounds L2697]. Partial pooling blends a market's estimate with the pooled one, weighted n / (n + k) in an invented rule, with k registered as a conjecture; borrowed parameters are recorded as borrowed [Borrowed Evidence L2701–2705].

### 17.4 Failure
  Borrowing Perrow's (1984) interactive complexity and tight coupling, coupled loops can oscillate where each alone is stable, so move bounds extend to joint quantities, revisions are staggered and reversals are monitored [Coupled Swings L2711–2713]. Following Reason, a stale feed is a latent condition; in an invented case it costs 16,000 a day, so freshness, range and second-source monitors belong in the specification [Broken Feeds L2717–2719]. Knight Capital's August 2012 deployment error, about 460 million dollars in 45 minutes, shows a latent flaw meeting an automated actor with no effective halt [Broken Feeds L2721]. Organizational rejection shows as a gap between engine and invoiced prices [Rollback Plans L2725]; rollback plans freeze last approved values, are rehearsed, and Klein's pre-mortem (2007) turns failures into owned conjectures [Rollback Plans L2727–2729].

### 18.2 Regulation
  The section reads external rules as an economist reads constraints on the feasible set, not as legal guidance [Price Differences L2788]. In the United States the Robinson-Patman Act (1936) prohibits discrimination in price between different purchasers of commodities of like grade and quality where the effect may be substantially to lessen competition, tend to create a monopoly, or injure competition with the seller, the favored buyer, or their customers, with defenses for cost-justified differences and good-faith meeting of a competitor's offer. In the statute, price discrimination means only a price difference between purchasers, unlawful where its effect may be to harm competition in one of those ways; that is not the economic sense Discrimination in Segmentation took from Pigou, in which prices differ across buyers or quantities with willingness to pay rather than cost, and the two meanings are often confused. Its reach to subscriptions and services is limited because it addresses commodities [Price Differences L2790]. Article 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union prohibits abuse of a dominant position, its listed examples including dissimilar conditions for equivalent transactions with other trading parties that place them at a competitive disadvantage, so a dominant firm's segment rules carry a burden of justification a smaller rival's would not [Price Differences L2790]. Price locks, most-favored-customer clauses and platform parity terms are recorded constraints on the cells they cover, with source and expiry [Price Differences L2792]; Article 5(3) of the Digital Markets Act (2022) bars designated gatekeepers from preventing business users from offering the same products or services to end users through third-party online intermediation services or their own direct online sales channels at prices or conditions different from those offered through the gatekeeper's online intermediation services [Price Differences L2792]. Conflicts with segment rules are resolved by Governance before the engine runs [Price Differences L2794].
  Section 1 of the Sherman Act (1890) prohibits contracts, combinations and conspiracies in restraint of trade, and Article 101 of the Treaty prohibits agreements between undertakings, decisions by associations of undertakings and concerted practices that may affect trade between Member States and have as their object or effect the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition; both turn on agreement or coordination between firms, so a unilateral meet-competition or most-favored-customer clause has been examined mainly for its effects, case by case, and a clause with a recorded purpose, band and owner reads differently in an inquiry [Coordination Law L2798]. Learned coordination without communication is possible in simulation, while the legal regimes still turn mainly on agreement, exchanged information or a common intermediary; the Department of Justice and several states sued RealPage in 2024, alleging its software let competing landlords share nonpublic data and align rents, and in November 2025 the Department filed a proposed settlement under which RealPage would stop using rivals' nonpublic data to set prices [Coordination Law L2800]. Pooled rival data through a vendor differs from reading public rival prices, so the input record shows which rival prices were read, from what source, and whether any came from rivals' private data [Coordination Law L2800]. The EU Omnibus Directive's 30-day prior-price rule turns an invented 45 offer after prices of 40 and 50 into a 12.5 percent rise, not a discount, unless the engine stores the 30-day minimum [Disclosure Duties L2804]; such rules are dated local constraints [Disclosure Duties L2806].

### 18.3 Accountability

The book notes GDPR Article 22 on solely automated decisions, leaves its application to counsel, and observes that review lanes can double as the required safeguard [Named Speakers L2818]. Override culture must keep the engine both stoppable and unbypassed: in an invented week, 7.5 percent overrides sit in band, while any bypass is traced before a class widens [Override Survival L2822–2824]; override clusters feed the double loop [Override Survival L2826]. Integrity at enterprise scale gains external readers in customers, regulators and boards, served from one record [Enterprise Integrity L2830–2834].
